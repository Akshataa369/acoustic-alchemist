An ordinary day

The small things are the ones you have to ask for

For people who can't easily use their hands, and can't always rely on their voice, the day is full of small moments of waiting. These moments are illustrations, not one person's story.

  1. 7:40 am

    The news finishes and the next video starts playing. It isn't one you wanted.

    With a sound: a short hiss, and it pauses.

  2. 11:15 am

    The e-book reaches the bottom of the page. The carer is busy in the kitchen.

    With a sound: a lip pop, and the page turns.

  3. 4:30 pm

    A friend sends photos from a wedding you couldn't go to. You'd like to look through them alone.

    With a sound: pop, pop, click. Forward, forward, back.

  4. 9:00 pm

    You want to listen to music, without asking anyone to stay up and press play.

    With a sound: a hum to start listening, and the evening is yours.

Technology keeps assuming a body that many people don't have

“Just use the mouse.”

Keyboards, mice and touchscreens need steady hands. Over 15 million people live with a spinal cord injury alone [3], and arthritis, tremors or a broken wrist make it hard for many more.

“Just say it out loud.”

Voice assistants need clear, steady speech. But the conditions that take away movement often take speech too. ALS slowly weakens the muscles used to speak [5].

“There's a device for that.”

There is, if you can get it. Eye trackers and switch devices exist, but access to assistive products is as low as 3% in poorer countries [2].

Our answer

It learns your voice. Not the other way round.

Most technology asks people to fit it. Acoustic-Alchemist listens to the sounds you can make, a pop, a click, a hum, however they come out, and learns them. A weak pop is fine. An unusual click is fine. It's your sound, and that's the point.

  • Any voice. Trained on your own recordings, in a few minutes.
  • Nothing to buy. An ordinary laptop microphone.
  • Private. Your sounds never leave your computer.
  • Careful. It only acts when it's sure, and one sound pauses it, so a cough in a meeting never flips a slide.
  • Joyful. The same sounds can play drums, because practice should feel like play.

      Why we built it

      We started out building a beatbox drum machine.

      It was fun. You could make a beat with your mouth. Then we asked a harder question: who would this matter to most? Not people who already have a hundred ways to use a computer, but people who have none that work for them.

      So we kept the drums, and gave the same sounds a second job: turning pages, pausing videos and moving slides, for someone who otherwise has to wait.

      Independence is made of small moments. We want to give a few of them back.

      Acoustic-Alchemist is a student prototype built in 10 days, not a medical device. Our next step is the most important one: building it with the people it's for, not just for them.
